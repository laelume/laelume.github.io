Thesis Title:

Computational methods towards vocal individuality of North island Brown Kiwi

Motivation

Automated individual identification (AIID) works towards recognizing which individual animal is calling from an acoustic recoding. Population monitoring has traditionally relied on physically observation and capture, which can be stressful or invasive for animals. Passive monitoring depends on call-count surveys that can't differentiate between multiple individuals in the same recording. Building an understanding of how to consistently measure an animal's acoustic signature could turn passive recorders into an individual census tool.

Prior work on kiwi AIID has shown that some aspects of individual identification are possible with CNNs. The open questions for my research are interested in which measurable acoustic elements of a call might carry which elements of individual identity. Open questions that shoudl be addressed in the process include per-individual signal stability, recording conditions, and generalizing from closed-set to open-set problems.

Dataset

  • Call sequences from several known individuals recorded in 2020 and 2021 using ABARs.
  • Call sequences from unknown individuals obtained through automated detection from PAM recordings.
  • Call sequences from unknkown individuals obtained from open source data repositories.
  • Several hundred annotated syllables from the above datasets. Each syllable has onset and offset times, and an estimated instantaneous frequency contour. A custom tool allows either the manual annotation of contours, or automated computation that can be corrected via user input.
  • 55 quantitative vocal metrics per syllable.
  • Future work will incorporate other species using publicly-available individual identity bird datasets for cross-species comparison.

Methods

Quantitative vocal metrics

Simple, interpretable numbers: durations, frequency bounds, bandwidths, trends, and harmonic intensity at the syllable level, plus timing and positional statistics at the sequence level. With 23 of these features, principal component analysis reduced to seven components followed by a nearest-neighbour or support-vector classifier reaches roughly 88 to 89 percent leave-one-out accuracy over five birds. Recursive feature elimination shows frequency bounds and duration doing most of the work. These metrics are the baseline every other method must beat, and the subject of the framework paper in progress.

Elastic shape analysis of contours

A kiwi syllable on a spectrogram is a curve. Two syllables from the same bird may be the same curve stretched or compressed in time. The square root velocity function (SRVF) framework from functional data analysis compares curves up to exactly that kind of re-timing, giving a distance that ignores tempo but keeps shape. This work computes elastic distance matrices between all pairs of contours (on the GPU, using an anti-diagonal wavefront dynamic programme), fits Karcher means, and encodes each contour as coordinates in a tangent-space principal component basis. A prototype-based dictionary was tried first and found inconclusive: contour shape varies continuously and resists discrete clusters, so a continuous shape code was adopted instead. The shape distances feed a support-vector machine with a precomputed kernel.

Syllable-type discovery

Before syntax can be studied, each syllable needs a label. Non-negative matrix factorisation on feature and spectrogram representations, together with UMAP embedding and density-based clustering, is used to discover recurring syllable types without supervision, and Hungarian matching on cosine similarity compares the discovered templates across birds. Pretrained bioacoustic models (Google's Perch, and CLAP for zero-shot probing) provide alternative embeddings for the same task.

Deep detectors for segmentation

Scaling beyond hand segmentation requires detectors. Two are in progress: a two-head network that finds inter-syllable gaps and syllable regions in raw field recordings, and a small recurrent network that finds frequency-jump changepoints inside a syllable. Both are designed around a domain constraint: not every silence is a syllable gap, and kiwi syllable spacing has no fixed duration, so the detectors must use context rather than thresholds.

Parametric synthesis

The third chapter inverts the problem. If a kiwi syllable is a rise, a plateau, and a fall, then a handful of sigmoid or Heaviside transitions with fitted times and slopes should reproduce it. Those fitted parameters become both a compact identity feature and the control knobs of a synthesiser. Fitting runs on the university cluster.

Evaluation discipline

Recordings from one call sequence are never split across training and test folds. Every classifier is evaluated with leave-one-group-out cross-validation grouped by sequence, and the individual bird is used as a grouping key, never as a leaked feature. This rule exists because an early cross-validation protocol was found to be at risk of sequence leakage, and the baseline numbers are only meaningful under the stricter protocol.
