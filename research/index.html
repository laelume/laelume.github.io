Individual Vocal Identity of Birds using Computational Bioacoustics

My current research covers a wide range of topics that live near the intersection of computational bioacoustics and AI. My PhD is based in New Zealand, where I'm applying my methodology towards understanding the vocal individuality of North Island Brown Kiwi (Apteryx mantelli). Along the way, I've designed a suite of software tools to make my life easier (or sometimes more difficult)!

Previously, I've worked as a performing musician, composer, sound designer, audio engineer, and many other things. Curiosity is the spice of life!

Overview:

Kiwi are nocturnal, shy, and somewhat rare to encounter in the wild. Conservation work in bioacoustics uses acoustic recorders deployed in the bush for Passive Acoustic Monitoring (PAM). Recording devices capture many hours of audio, including the calls of individual birds, amongst many other sounds! If a bird's identity could be determined from a wild field recording, it would be possible to count and track kiwi populations without catching or tagging them.

My PhD research involves understanding the shape and structure of kiwi syllables and sequences, how individuality can be measured from a various representations of the audio, and overall, how maths and AI can be used to describe and detect patterns in sound structures.

Proposed contributions

  • Describe and classify syllablesDiscover the syllable types each bird uses, and describe each syllable using custom quantitative vocal metrics and elastic shape analysis of its frequency contour.
  • Read the syntax of a callUsing discovered syllable labels, look for patterns in how syllables are sequenced, and detect changepoints in the sequence structure.
  • Synthesize realistic callsBuild procedural and hybrid generative models of kiwi calls, and use them to design playback experiments to be conducted in the field.

Dataset

The preliminary dataset comes from a small collection of wild recordings taken in 2020 and 2021 from Animal-Borne Autonomous Recorders (ABARS). From those recordings, I annotated several hundred syllables using my custom annotation software, YAAAT. Then I computed the instantaneous frequency contours for each syllable. Since instantaneous frequency algorithms are prone to noise, trimming the audio to the syllable boundaries yields more accurate results. I also defined a set of Quantitative Vocal Metrics (QVM) to characterize a variety of time-frequency properties of the syllables.

The initial baseline for classification was pretty good. Principal component analysis with nearest-neighbour and support-vector classification gives 87-93% accuracy for distinguishing individual identity. The majority of my work is focused on explaining how and why those results hold, and diving deeply into the nuances of vocal individuality. Additional goals include making my methodology robust to leakage between recordings, and to scale for larger datasets and other species.

Software

My bioacoustics software suite is entitled ANVO (Animal Vocalizations), which is structured as a collection of Python packages. The projects page attempt to organize them conceptually.

  • Sequences and Syllables: various tasks in sound event detection, syllable and sequence identitfication, phoneme and transitional regions within syllables.
  • Identity and description: vocal metrics registry, shape analysis, syllable prototype discovery, identity distribution analysis, classifiers that turn a syllable into an identity estimate.
  • Synthesis and generation: parametric physical model for vocal production of birds, DDSP, genetic, and gradient descent parameter optimization for sound matching.
  • Exploratory methods: topological data analysis, attractor reconstruction, source separation.
