Individual Vocal Identity using Computational Bioacoustics?

In my PhD research, I'm exploring a wide variety of topics in computational bioacoustics. One main theme has been on the individual vocal identity of avian species. My PhD is based in New Zealand, where I'm applying my methodology to understand the vocal individuality of North Island Brown Kiwi (Apteryx mantelli). Along the way, I've designed a suite of software tools to make my life easier (or sometimes more difficult)!

An example of a frequency contours for a synthetic male kiwi call as seen on a spectrogram: three syllables (blue contours) with frequency modulation that rises, holds, and falls. Green ticks mark syllable onsets and offsets, yellow dots mark theoretical changepoints inside a syllable, and pink bands mark the gaps between syllables. This forms the basis of our avian bioacosutics framework.

Overview:

Kiwi are nocturnal, shy, and relatively rare to encounter in the wild. Conservation work in Aotearoa New Zealand relies heavily on acoustic recorders deployed in the bush for Passive Acoustic Monitoring (PAM). These devices capture hours of audio, including the calls of individual birds. If the identity of the calling bird could be read from the call alone, it would be possible to count and track kiwi populations without catching or tagging them.

My PhD research concerns understanding the shape of kiwi frequency contours, individualty measurable within the calls, and how to describe those structures mathematically.

Intended contributions

A handful of intended contributions form the arc of the thesis.

  • Describe and classify syllablesDiscover the recurring syllable types each bird uses, and describe each syllable with quantitative vocal metrics and elastic shape analysis of its frequency contour.
  • Read the syntax of a callUsing the discovered syllable labels, look for patterns in how syllables are sequenced, and detect changepoints in the sequence structure.
  • Synthesise realistic callsBuild procedural and hybrid generative models of kiwi calls, with an eye toward future playback experiments in the field.

What the data looks like

The preliminary dataset comes from a small collection of wild recordings taken in 2020 and 2021 from Animal-Borne Autonomous Recorders (ABARS). From those recordings, several hundred syllables have been segmented by hand. Each syllable has onset and offset times, a frequency contour with annotated changepoints, and 55 quantitative vocal metrics.

A first baseline already exists: principal component analysis followed by nearest-neighbour and support-vector classification reaches low-90s percent accuracy at telling the birds apart. Much of the current work aims to explain why that works, make it robust to leakage between recordings, and scale it beyond the test population to real and noisy recordings.

How the projects fit together

The software is organised as a family of sibling Python packages under the ANVO umbrella (ANimal VOcalization), each with one job. The projects page groups them by the part of the pipeline they serve:

  • Finding calls and syllables: detecting kiwi in long recordings, locating the gaps between syllables, and marking the changepoints inside a syllable.
  • Identity and description: the metrics registry, shape analysis, prototype discovery, and classifiers that turn a syllable into an identity guess.
  • Synthesis and generation: parametric pitch models and synthetic datasets that make realistic kiwi calls from a handful of numbers.
  • Tools and infrastructure: the annotation tool, audio library, notebook utilities, documentation, and compute setup that keep everything reproducible.
  • Exploratory methods: topological data analysis, attractor reconstruction, and source separation, tried because they might reveal structure the standard methods miss.
